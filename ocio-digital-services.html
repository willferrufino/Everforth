Digital services that are secure, reliable, and easier to use

A plain-language example of how a federal technology office can explain its work to the public without relying on technical jargon or internal organizational language.

What this office does

Technology supports nearly every service the Department provides. In this portfolio example, the Office of the Chief Information Officer helps teams use technology in a way that is secure, dependable, accessible, and focused on the people who use government services.

Improve digital services

Help teams make websites and digital tools easier to understand, navigate, and use on different devices and with assistive technology.

Support secure technology

Promote reliable technology practices that help protect information and keep essential digital services available.

Set shared standards

Use common approaches for accessibility, content quality, web publishing, and technology governance so teams can deliver more consistent experiences.

What people should be able to do

Federal web content should help people quickly find information, understand what it means, and know what to do next. This page uses short sections, descriptive headings, active voice, and familiar words to make scanning easier.

Plain-language goal

Lead with the information people need. Explain technical work in everyday terms. Define an acronym before using it, and avoid one when the full term is clearer.

Accessibility is part of the content

Accessibility is not a final publishing step. Writers, content managers, designers, and developers all contribute to an accessible experience.

  • Use a logical heading structure so screen-reader users can navigate the page.
  • Write link text that explains the destination instead of using “click here.”
  • Provide useful text alternatives for meaningful images.
  • Use labels and instructions for form fields.
  • Do not rely on color alone to communicate status or meaning.

How content stays current

Each page should have a known owner, a review date, and a clear path for updating or retiring content. A simple content governance process helps reduce outdated information, duplicate pages, broken links, and inconsistent guidance.

What this sample demonstrates

Federal plain language

Audience-first structure, concise sentences, active voice, descriptive headings, limited acronyms, and clear calls to action.

WCAG / Section 508 thinking

Semantic headings, keyboard focus, accessible navigation, meaningful links, responsive design, contrast-conscious UI, and reduced-motion support.

Content governance

Visible ownership, review dates, content lifecycle language, and links into QA and governance artifacts.
